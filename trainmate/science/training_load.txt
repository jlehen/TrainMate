TRAINING LOAD MONITORING GUIDELINES (FITNESS/FATIGUE MODEL)
===========================================================

Training load monitoring answers three related questions:
  (a) Where is the athlete on the fitness / fatigue / freshness curve, and how
      should load progress week over week and taper into an event?  -> PMC (CTL/ATL/TSB)
  (b) Is the multi-week climb sustainable?                          -> CTL ramp rate
  (c) Is fatigue spiking relative to THIS athlete's own base?       -> ATL:CTL ratio

All three operate on the same underlying daily training load.
   - Load metric (per session): TSS, or (RPE * Duration) when power/pace is
     unavailable. Sum per calendar day to get daily load.


1. FITNESS / FATIGUE / FORM (PERFORMANCE MANAGEMENT CHART, PMC)
---------------------------------------------------------------
The PMC (Banister impulse-response model) tracks three exponentially-weighted
moving averages of daily load. It captures accumulated fitness, short-term
fatigue, and the balance between them ("form").

   - CTL (Chronic Training Load) = "Fitness"
       Exponentially-weighted moving average of daily load, ~42-day time
       constant. Rises slowly; represents accumulated fitness.
   - ATL (Acute Training Load) = "Fatigue"
       Exponentially-weighted moving average of daily load, ~7-day time
       constant. Rises and falls quickly; represents recent fatigue.
   - TSB (Training Stress Balance) = "Form" = CTL(yesterday) - ATL(yesterday)
       Positive = fresh/tapered; negative = fatigued/loaded.

   TSB interpretation (approximate bands):
   - TSB > +25:        Very fresh but detraining risk if sustained (fitness
                       decaying). Only appropriate right before an A event.
   - TSB +5 to +25:    Peaked / race-ready freshness. Target band for an A-event
                       taper.
   - TSB -10 to +5:    Neutral / "grey zone". Maintenance or light build.
   - TSB -10 to -30:   Productive overload. Normal and desirable inside a build
                       block: fitness is being built while fatigue is carried.
   - TSB < -30:        Excessive fatigue. High overreaching / injury risk;
                       insert recovery.

   IMPORTANT LIMIT: TSB is a DIFFERENCE in absolute load units, so these bands
   are only calibrated for one fitness level. See §3.


2. WEEK-OVER-WEEK PROGRESSION: CTL RAMP RATE
--------------------------------------------
"How much to increase load week over week" is governed by the CTL ramp rate:
the weekly rise in CTL, expressed in load units per day per week (e.g. TSS/day
per week).

   - Ramp rate ~3-5:   Sustainable for most athletes building fitness.
   - Ramp rate ~5-7:   Aggressive; acceptable for experienced athletes in a
                       focused build block, monitored closely.
   - Ramp rate > 8:    High risk of non-functional overreaching / injury.
                       Avoid outside short, deliberate overload weeks.

   As a volume heuristic, this aligns with limiting weekly volume increases to
   roughly 5-10% (the classic "10% rule" is a soft ceiling, not a validated
   law). Ramp on load, not on a fixed percentage, and back off when recovery
   metrics or TSB indicate the athlete is not absorbing the load.

   Ramp rate is also absolute, and carries the same caveat as TSB: a ramp of +5
   is ~5%/week on a CTL of 100 but ~25%/week on a CTL of 20. Scale the ceiling
   down for a low-CTL athlete rather than reading the raw number.


3. RELATIVE OVERLOAD: THE ATL:CTL RATIO
---------------------------------------
   ATL:CTL = ATL / CTL

Fatigue measured against the athlete's OWN fitness base, rather than in
absolute load units. This is the scale-invariant companion to TSB, and it
exists because TSB's fixed bands quietly mean different things at different
fitness levels. What ATL has to reach for TSB to hit -30:

     CTL  15  ->  ATL  45  ->  ATL:CTL 3.0
     CTL  30  ->  ATL  60  ->  ATL:CTL 2.0
     CTL  60  ->  ATL  90  ->  ATL:CTL 1.5
     CTL 100  ->  ATL 130  ->  ATL:CTL 1.3

   The same "excessive fatigue" trigger therefore fires at a 1.3x overload for
   a well-trained athlete and only at a 3.0x overload for someone returning
   from a layoff — that is, it goes permissive exactly when the athlete is most
   fragile. Worked example: an athlete back from a layoff at CTL 15 does one
   enthusiastic 250 TSS week. ATL climbs to ~32, so TSB reads -17 ("productive
   overload, normal") while ATL:CTL reads 2.1. The ratio is right and TSB is
   wrong. Lean on the ratio whenever CTL is low: early base, post-injury
   return, off-season restart.

   Interpretation:
   - ~1.0:             Steady state. Acute load matches the chronic base.
   - 1.0 - 1.3:        Normal building or maintenance.
   - 1.3 - 1.5:        Elevated relative overload. Sustainable only briefly and
                       only when deliberate; cross-check recovery metrics.
   - > 1.5:            Acute load has spiked far above the athlete's base.
                       Reduce load unless this is a planned, capped overload.
   - < 1.0:            Unloading. Deliberate in a taper, deload, or a
                       low-volume intensity block; a sign of drift or lost
                       consistency only when NOT planned. See §4 — a low ratio
                       is not by itself a fault to correct.

   Note on ACWR: the classic acute:chronic workload ratio (7-day load over the
   28-day weekly average) measures the same thing over flat windows. It is not
   used here. Flat windows produce step artifacts when a single big session
   falls out of the 7-day window, the acute window sits inside its own chronic
   window by construction, and the published 0.8-1.3 "sweet spot" comes from
   team-sport injury surveillance rather than planned endurance training. The
   ATL:CTL ratio gives the same relative-overload signal on the smoother EWMAs
   the PMC already computes.


4. PLANNED vs UNPLANNED: READING THESE INSIDE A BLOCK
-----------------------------------------------------
TSB and ATL:CTL detect UNINTENDED load. They cannot, by themselves, tell a
deliberate training decision apart from a mistake — and periodization is built
out of deliberate load changes, especially block periodization, whose whole
mechanism is concentrated loading and sharp transitions.

   Judge the athlete's numbers against the PLAN, not against a universal band.
   A value that matches its planned trajectory is on plan, whatever band it
   falls in. A value that departs from the planned trajectory is the signal.

   Expected trajectories (see periodization.txt for the block definitions):

   - Accumulation / high-volume block: ATL:CTL steps up on entry (typically
     1.2-1.4) and settles back toward 1.1 as CTL catches up. TSB sits in the
     -10 to -30 productive-overload band. Do NOT trim planned volume merely
     because the ratio is elevated; that sands the step off and turns block
     periodization into a linear ramp.
   - Transmutation / intensity block: volume drops sharply while intensity
     steps up, so ATL:CTL falls to ~0.7-0.9 and TSB rises. This is the block
     working as designed. Do NOT add volume back to "correct" the low ratio —
     the block's effect depends on staying concentrated and low-volume.
   - Realization / taper: load is cut hard. ATL:CTL falls to ~0.6-0.8 and TSB
     rises into the +5 to +25 target band. A low ratio here is the goal.
   - Deload week: ATL:CTL dips to ~0.7-0.9 for the week by design.
   - Unstructured, off-plan, or returning from a layoff/illness: no planned
     trajectory exists to compare against, so read the bands in §1 and §3
     directly. This is where an unplanned spike is genuinely dangerous and
     where the ratio earns its keep.

   When actual load departs from planned load, the DEVIATION is what matters:
   a ratio of 1.44 against a planned 1.40 is on plan; a ratio of 1.44 against a
   planned 1.05 is an unplanned spike and should be treated as one.


5. COACHING DIRECTIVES
----------------------
   - Build CTL gradually via ramp rate, and keep ATL:CTL near 1.0-1.3 outside
     deliberate overload blocks.
   - Establish which phase the athlete is in BEFORE reading any band. Inside a
     declared block, compare against the §4 expected trajectory. Outside one,
     read the bands directly.
   - Treat an UNPLANNED ATL:CTL above 1.5, or a ramp rate above 8, as a caution
     even when the other looks fine — they can diverge. A single big week can
     spike the ratio while ramp stays fine; a smooth relentless build can hold
     the ratio near 1.1 while ramp exceeds 8.
   - Do NOT treat a low ATL:CTL as a fault to correct without first checking
     whether it is planned (taper, deload, intensity block). If it is planned,
     hold the plan.
   - When TSB falls below -30, default to recovery/deload and override
     continued loading — unless this is a short, deliberate overreach block,
     in which case cap its duration and watch recovery metrics closely.
   - Scale the TSB and ramp thresholds down for a low-CTL athlete, and lean on
     ATL:CTL instead: at CTL under ~30 the absolute bands are too permissive
     (§3).
   - To peak for an A event, taper load so TSB rises into the +5 to +25 band by
     event day while preserving intensity (see periodization deload/realization
     protocol).
   - Sustained TSB well above +25 means fitness is decaying: resume building —
     unless the athlete is inside a planned realization/taper block, where it
     is the intended state.
   - Cross-check all of the above against recovery metrics (HRV, RHR, sleep);
     depressed recovery overrides a "safe" reading on any of these models, and
     is the abort signal that outranks a planned block.
